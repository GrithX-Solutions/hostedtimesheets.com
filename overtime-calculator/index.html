Quick answer

This overtime pay calculator separates regular hours from overtime hours, then applies your overtime multiplier to the hourly rate. For covered nonexempt U.S. employees, the Fair Labor Standards Act generally requires at least 1.5× the regular rate after 40 hours in a workweek. The calculator defaults to 40 hours and 1.5×, but both values remain adjustable.

Overtime hours = max(0, total hours − overtime threshold)

Overtime rate = regular hourly rate × overtime multiplier

Estimated gross pay = regular pay + overtime pay

Example: 45 hours at $25/hour with a 40-hour threshold and 1.5× multiplier produces 40 regular hours, 5 overtime hours, a $37.50 overtime rate and $1,187.50 estimated gross pay.

Time-and-a-half overtime formula

Time-and-a-half means the overtime hourly rate equals 1.5 times the regular hourly rate. At $20 per hour, the time-and-a-half rate is $30 per overtime hour. At $25 per hour, it is $37.50.

Time-and-a-half rate = regular hourly rate × 1.5

Overtime rate versus overtime premium

The overtime rate is the full hourly amount paid for an overtime hour. The overtime premium is only the extra amount above straight time. For a $20 regular rate, a $30 time-and-a-half rate contains a $10 premium. This calculator shows the full overtime rate and overtime pay so you do not add the premium twice.

Methodology and assumptions

The calculator uses only the values you enter. It does not decide whether overtime is legally required, which hours qualify, whether daily overtime applies, or whether bonuses, commissions or differentials change the regular rate. Use applicable payroll rules and authoritative records for those determinations.

The regular hourly rate field should contain the regular rate you intend to use for this estimate. Under the FLSA, the statutory regular rate can include more than a stated base wage, so a one-rate calculator may not fit workers with includable bonuses, commissions, differentials or multiple rates.

U.S. references: DOL Fact Sheet #23 covers federal overtime requirements, and DOL Fact Sheet #56A explains the regular rate of pay. State laws and worker-specific exemptions can differ.

How overtime pay is calculated

Start by separating total hours into regular hours and overtime hours using the threshold you enter.

Regular hours = min(total hours, overtime threshold)

Overtime hours = max(0, total hours − overtime threshold)

Then calculate the regular and overtime pay components.

Regular pay = regular hours × regular hourly rate

Overtime rate = regular hourly rate × overtime multiplier

Overtime pay = overtime hours × overtime rate

Estimated gross pay = regular pay + overtime pay

Effective hourly rate = estimated gross pay ÷ total hours

If total hours are zero, the calculator displays a $0.00 effective hourly rate instead of dividing by zero.

Example

If you work 45 hours at $25 per hour, use a 40-hour threshold and a 1.5× multiplier, the calculator treats 40 hours as regular time and 5 hours as overtime. The overtime rate is $37.50 per hour.

Why the threshold is configurable

Overtime rules can differ by jurisdiction, employer policy, worker classification and work arrangement. This tool therefore does not assume that a particular threshold or multiplier applies to everyone.

What gross pay means here

Estimated gross pay is regular pay plus overtime pay before taxes, deductions, benefits, bonuses or other adjustments.

Gross overtime pay vs. the federal qualified overtime deduction

This calculator is not a tax-deduction calculator. It estimates gross regular and overtime pay before taxes. Current IRS guidance treats the federal qualified overtime deduction as a separate calculation that applies only to qualifying overtime compensation above the regular rate and has its own eligibility, reporting and limit rules.

Current U.S. tax reference: IRS Fact Sheet FS-2026-13 (August 2026). For ordinary FLSA time-and-a-half, IRS guidance describes the qualified amount as the premium above the regular rate rather than the entire overtime payment. Verify tax eligibility and reporting separately.

Frequently asked questions

What multiplier should I use?

Use the multiplier that applies under your employer policy, agreement or applicable rules. For covered nonexempt U.S. employees, federal overtime is generally at least 1.5× the regular rate after 40 hours in a workweek, but state rules and exemptions can differ.

Can I calculate overtime with decimal hours?

Yes. For example, 42 hours 30 minutes can be entered as 42.5 hours.

Does this include taxes or deductions?

No. The gross-pay estimate is before taxes, deductions and other payroll adjustments.

Can I use this as a payroll and overtime calculator?

Yes. For a simple hourly payroll with one base rate, one overtime threshold and one multiplier, it separates regular pay from overtime pay and estimates gross pay before taxes and deductions.

Is overtime always after 40 hours in a week?

For covered nonexempt employees under the U.S. Fair Labor Standards Act, overtime is generally due after 40 hours in a workweek. State laws, exemptions and special rules can change the applicable calculation.

Are weekends or holidays automatically overtime?

Not under the federal FLSA simply because the work occurs on a weekend or holiday. State law, an agreement or employer policy may provide different premium-pay rules.

Does this calculate the federal no-tax-on-overtime deduction?

No. This tool estimates gross overtime pay only. Current IRS guidance treats the qualified overtime deduction as a separate tax calculation with its own eligibility, reporting and limits.

Overtime estimate

Separate total hours, overtime classification and pay rate

An overtime estimate has three distinct parts: how many hours were worked, which of those hours fall above the threshold you are using, and what rate applies to those classified hours.

Start with a verified total-hours figure

Use hours that have already been checked for clock-in/out errors, unpaid breaks and missing punches. The overtime calculator assumes the total hours you enter are correct; it does not reconstruct a time card from raw punches.

The threshold is configurable because rules differ

If you enter a 40-hour threshold and 46 total hours, the arithmetic split is 40 regular and 6 overtime hours. A different threshold produces a different classification. The tool does not determine which threshold applies to a worker, employer or jurisdiction, and it does not model every possible daily or industry-specific rule.

Overtime pay uses the multiplier you enter

At a $20 base hourly rate and a 1.5 multiplier, the overtime rate is $30 per hour. Six overtime hours would therefore contribute $180 of estimated overtime pay. Regular pay for 40 hours at $20 is $800, producing a simple estimated gross total of $980.

Multiple rates and premiums need a more specific payroll calculation

This calculator uses one regular hourly rate and one overtime multiplier. Employees with multiple job rates, bonuses that affect the regular rate, shift differentials, commissions or special premium rules may require a different calculation. Do not force those cases into a one-rate estimate.

Keep gross-pay math separate from the federal overtime tax deduction

The calculator estimates gross pay and does not calculate federal income tax or the qualified overtime deduction. In 2026 search results, tax-deduction calculators appear alongside ordinary overtime-pay calculators, but they answer a different question. Use current IRS guidance for qualified-overtime eligibility, reporting and deduction limits.

Rounding should be resolved before overtime classification

If the source timekeeping system rounds punches or daily totals, use the resulting approved hours consistently. Small rounding differences near an overtime threshold can change the classified hours, so avoid mixing raw punches with already-rounded totals in the same calculation.

The result is an arithmetic estimate, not a legal determination of overtime eligibility or required pay. Compare payroll calculations with the applicable rules, employer policy and authoritative records before finalizing wages.
