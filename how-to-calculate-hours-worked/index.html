How to Calculate Hours Worked

To calculate hours worked, subtract clock-in from clock-out, then subtract any unpaid break time. If the shift crosses midnight, treat the end time as the following day. Add each day's net worked time for a weekly total, and divide leftover minutes by 60 when you need decimal hours.

Quick answer: the hours worked formula

The basic calculation is:

Hours worked = clock-out time − clock-in time − unpaid breaks

For example, a shift from 8:30 AM to 5:15 PM lasts 8 hours 45 minutes. Subtract a 30-minute unpaid lunch and the net worked time is 8 hours 15 minutes, or 8.25 decimal hours.

If you only need the answer, use the Work Hours Calculator. The rest of this guide explains how the calculation works, including lunch breaks, overnight shifts, split shifts, weekly totals and decimal conversion.

Step 1: find the elapsed time between clock-in and clock-out

Start with the full span of the shift before breaks. If you clock in at 9:00 AM and clock out at 5:30 PM, the elapsed shift is 8 hours 30 minutes.

Clock inClock outElapsed shift
8:00 AM4:30 PM8h 30m
8:30 AM5:15 PM8h 45m
9:00 AM5:30 PM8h 30m
7:45 AM4:00 PM8h 15m

This is gross elapsed time, not necessarily paid or net worked time. The next step is to account for breaks that should be excluded from the timesheet.

Step 2: subtract unpaid lunch or break time

Subtract only break time that should be treated as unpaid for the record you are preparing. If a break is paid, or if your timekeeping system has already removed it, do not subtract it a second time.

Example: a 9:00 AM to 5:30 PM shift is 8 hours 30 minutes. With a 30-minute unpaid lunch:

8h 30m − 30m = 8h 00m worked

With a 45-minute unpaid lunch:

8h 30m − 45m = 7h 45m worked

For a full weekly time card with daily lunch deductions, use the Timesheet Calculator With Lunch Break.

Step 3: handle overnight shifts correctly

An overnight shift has a clock-out time that looks earlier than the clock-in time because it ends on the next calendar day. Do not subtract the times as if they happened on the same date.

Example: clock in at 10:00 PM and clock out at 6:00 AM. The elapsed shift is 8 hours. If there is a 30-minute unpaid break:

8h 00m − 30m = 7h 30m worked

A reliable way to think about the math is to add 24 hours to the end time whenever clock-out is earlier than clock-in. Our work-hours and timesheet calculators handle that rollover automatically.

Step 4: add split shifts separately

Some workdays have two or more work periods. Instead of treating the full day as one continuous shift, calculate each work period and add them together.

Example:

Work periodTimeWorked
Shift 18:00 AM–12:00 PM4h 00m
Shift 21:00 PM–5:00 PM4h 00m
Total—8h 00m

This avoids accidentally counting the one-hour gap between shifts as worked time. The Weekly Timesheet Calculator supports two work periods per day.

A minutes-first method that is easy to audit

When manual subtraction feels error-prone, convert each clock time to minutes after midnight. This keeps the arithmetic in whole minutes until the final conversion.

Minutes after midnight = (hour × 60) + minutes

For an overnight shift, add 1,440 minutes to clock-out before subtracting. Then calculate:

Net worked minutes = adjusted clock-out minutes − clock-in minutes − unpaid break minutes

Decimal hours = net worked minutes ÷ 60

Example: 10:00 PM is 1,320 minutes. A 6:00 AM next-day clock-out is 360 + 1,440 = 1,800 minutes. The elapsed shift is 480 minutes. After a 30-minute break, net worked time is 450 minutes, or 7h 30m and 7.50 decimal hours.

How to calculate hours worked in Excel or Google Sheets

Use real spreadsheet time values for clock-in and clock-out. Suppose A2 is clock-in, B2 is clock-out, and C2 contains unpaid break minutes as a number such as 30.

Decimal hours, including overnight shifts

=MOD(B2-A2,1)*24-(C2/60)

MOD(...,1) wraps a negative time difference across midnight. Multiplying by 24 converts the spreadsheet's fraction of a day into decimal hours. The break minutes are divided by 60 before subtraction.

Hours and minutes instead of decimals

=MOD(B2-A2,1)-(C2/1440)

Format that result as a duration such as [h]:mm. Dividing break minutes by 1,440 converts them to the fraction-of-a-day format spreadsheets use for time arithmetic.

Weekly total

If daily decimal-hour results are in D2:D8, use =SUM(D2:D8). Keep the daily source rows intact so you can trace the weekly result back to each shift instead of correcting only the final total.

These formulas perform time arithmetic only. They do not decide whether a break should be paid, how punches should be rounded, or which hours qualify for overtime.

Formula limits: These time-only formulas assume one shift shorter than 24 hours. When clock-in and clock-out are identical, MOD returns zero; it cannot tell a zero-length entry from a full-day shift. Use dated timestamps for multi-day work, and verify missing or ambiguous punches before calculating. For Excel time-value details, see Microsoft's time calculation guide.

How to calculate hours worked using a 24-hour clock

The arithmetic is the same in 24-hour time. Convert both times to minutes or use spreadsheet time values. For example, 07:45 to 16:20 is 8h 35m elapsed. Subtract a 35-minute break and the net worked time is exactly 8h 00m, or 8.00 decimal hours.

Do not enter a clock time such as 16:20 as the decimal number 16.20. Clock notation and decimal hours are different representations.

What to do with missing or ambiguous punches

Do not silently turn a missing clock-out into midnight, zero, or a full-day shift. Mark the row incomplete and verify the missing time before calculating it. Likewise, identical clock-in and clock-out times can mean zero elapsed time or a full-day span depending on the record; the timestamps or surrounding schedule must resolve that ambiguity.

How to calculate weekly hours worked

Calculate each day after subtracting that day's unpaid breaks, then add the daily net totals. Do not subtract the same breaks again from the weekly total.

How to convert worked time to decimal hours

Timesheets, payroll systems and invoices often use decimal hours. The digits after the decimal point are fractions of an hour, not clock minutes.

Decimal hours = whole hours + (minutes ÷ 60)

A common mistake is writing 8 hours 30 minutes as 8.30. That is incorrect: 0.30 of an hour is 18 minutes. For exact conversions, use the Decimal Hours Calculator or Hours & Minutes Calculator. For a full lookup table and spreadsheet formulas, see the Minutes to Decimal Hours Conversion Chart.

Worked hours, paid hours and payroll hours are related but not identical

Worked hours usually describe the time remaining after excluded breaks are removed. Paid hours can depend on how a specific employer treats breaks, leave, minimum guarantees or other paid time. Payroll hours often describe the numeric format sent into payroll, frequently as decimal hours.

For that reason, do the time arithmetic first and keep policy decisions separate. If you already know the daily hours and minutes and simply need a payroll-ready weekly total, use the Payroll Hours Calculator.

Break treatment is not a universal deduction: In the United States, the U.S. Department of Labor's Fact Sheet #22 explains general federal distinctions between short rest breaks and bona fide meal periods. Check the applicable records and rules before excluding any time; this guide does not decide compensability or overtime eligibility.

Common mistakes when calculating work hours

1. Treating clock minutes as decimal fractions

8:30 is 8.50 decimal hours, not 8.30.

2. Subtracting a break twice

If clock-in/clock-out records already exclude a meal period because the worker clocked out and back in, do not also subtract the same break as a separate deduction.

3. Ignoring midnight

10:00 PM to 6:00 AM is an 8-hour elapsed shift, not a negative result.

4. Counting the gap in a split shift

Calculate each work period separately so non-working gaps are not added to the total.

5. Rounding too early

Keep calculations in minutes for as long as possible. Convert or round the final result according to the timekeeping or payroll method you actually need.

6. Mixing time calculation with overtime rules

First determine total hours worked. Then apply the overtime threshold or policy relevant to the situation. Keeping those steps separate makes the calculation easier to audit.

Manual calculation vs a work hours calculator

Manual calculation is useful when checking one shift or auditing a result. A calculator becomes more useful when you have several days, overnight work, different break lengths, decimal conversions or a weekly total.

For one repeated schedule, use the Work Hours Calculator. For daily clock-in and clock-out entries, use the Weekly Timesheet Calculator. For a weekly time card that specifically deducts lunch breaks, use the Timesheet Calculator With Lunch Break.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate hours worked between two times?

Subtract clock-in from clock-out to get elapsed time, then subtract any unpaid break minutes. If the shift crosses midnight, treat clock-out as occurring on the following day.

How do I calculate work hours with a lunch break?

Find the elapsed shift first, then subtract only the lunch or break time that should be excluded. For example, 9:00 AM to 5:30 PM is 8 hours 30 minutes; minus a 30-minute break equals 8 hours.

How do I calculate 8 hours 30 minutes in decimal form?

Divide 30 minutes by 60 to get 0.50, then add 8. The result is 8.50 decimal hours.

How do I calculate an overnight shift?

If clock-out is earlier than clock-in, count clock-out as the next day. A 10:00 PM to 6:00 AM shift is 8 elapsed hours before breaks.

How do I calculate a weekly timesheet?

Calculate each day's net worked time after excluded breaks, then add the daily totals. Convert the final minutes to decimal hours only if your timesheet or payroll system requires decimals.

What Excel formula calculates hours worked with a break?

If A2 is clock-in, B2 is clock-out, and C2 is unpaid break minutes, use =MOD(B2-A2,1)*24-(C2/60) for decimal hours. MOD handles an overnight shift.

Can I use the same formula in Google Sheets?

Yes. Google Sheets also stores times as fractions of a day, so =MOD(B2-A2,1)*24-(C2/60) works for decimal hours when A2 and B2 contain valid times and C2 contains break minutes.

Should overtime be included when calculating hours worked?

Treat total worked-time arithmetic and overtime classification as separate steps. First calculate the worked-time total, then apply the threshold or policy that actually applies to the situation.
